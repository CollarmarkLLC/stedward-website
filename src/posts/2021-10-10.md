## From The Font

_Jesus answered him, "Why do you call me good? No one is good but God alone.”_

Biblical Fundamentalists can be deeply unnerved by a quotation like this! Read without context, these words of Jesus are damning for anyone who believes that He, Himself, is God.

Outside of the limiting scope of bad theology, these words of Jesus are simply an example of the Lord meeting different people where they, themselves, happen to be. To an educated man, Jesus might propose a lofty thought experiment. To a grieving widow, He might tell a parable of hope. To a cocky tax collector, He might give a fiery sermon. In each case, the specific theological or religious theme draws more details into the broader picture of Christianity. BUT! Read without a broader context and misunderstood as some kind of complete instruction book for life, the Bible can become a complete mess of seemingly contradictory and confused messages.

Of course, so would Shakespeare. The whole point of my favorite of his plays - Much Ado About Nothing - is that each character in the story misunderstands what’s going on and it’s only by reading all their misunderstandings TOGETHER that the play makes any sense at all. The brilliant Victorian author Jane Austin wrote her own version of Much Ado About Nothing which she titled Pride & Prejudice. Her version makes my point even more eloquently as the reader is misdirected by not two, but three relationships all of which weave a wonderfully convoluted story.

The story of Christianity is not an amalgam of a thousand isolated “proof-texts” meant to be sorted into weaponized categories. The Bible is a record of lived experiences and we have to read the whole story as one complete, integral revelation.

And so when Jesus speaks to this apparently devout and pious little guy who claims to have kept all the commandments for his entire life, Jesus speaks to him as one would speak to a spiritual master! Jesus doesn’t hold back and doesn’t wrap His message in a parable or soft language. He tells the man to become what we would call a monk! He gave this guy a chance to be Francis of Assisi 1000 years early!

Read in context, this story makes perfect sense. Read in isolation, it’s as useless as reading only Mr. Darcy’s parts of Austin’s novel.

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

This Wednesday is our parish Patronal Feast! St. Edward the Confessor was celebrated on October 13 from the 11th century until the 1960s.

Edward began his life in exile. England was being run by a line of Danish kings, the most famous of which was Cnut the Great. Edward and his siblings were exiles living in various European royal courts. While Edward’s brother had a chance of one day becoming king of England, Edward really didn’t. He was far enough down the list that he didn’t really have any special ambition in his youth.

His mother, though, was a brilliant tactician and managed to work some political magic to get Edward the big chair. He inherited a politically stable, economically stable, and culturally stable nation. The good side of that was that there was little for Edward to do except keep on keeping on. The bad side of that was that there was little to nothing Edward could do as king without upsetting the apple cart. Rather than indulging the worst parts of power, Edward invested in the Church and in his own spiritual life. He built churches and monasteries. He sought civil and private advice from bishops and the pope. He attended Mass almost daily. He was a deeply concerned and involved father.

Edward’s reign was all sunshine and rainbows, but he ruled in a time of peace and prosperity and built, as a legacy, Westminster Abbey which was the centerpiece of his effort to move the seat of government from the City of London (which isn’t the same as the city (small c) of London) to a nearby area on the west side of the river, hence the name “Westminster.” His innovations in that time are still in place 1000 years later. The other civil reason for which Edward is remembered so fondly in England is simply that his son was a bad king who spent less than a year on the throne before losing one of the most important and famous battles in history at Hastings in 1066.

All the civil history aside, the reason that Edward is a saint doesn’t have much to do with his investments in the Church or his earthly life at all. Edward is a saint because almost immediately after his death, people visiting the tomb began to experience miraculous healings. IF his son had been the kind while this was happening, we could chalk it up to propaganda, but most of the miracles took place under the reign of the new French kings who came from the Brittany region of France and who give us the island the name Great Brittain. The so-called British kings, as opposed to Cnut’s Danish kings, had no incentive at all to make a saint of their fallen adversary and yet the miracles were so prevalent that even the French line of English kings accepted that it was from the Lord.

Edward’s life was surely saintly, but his afterlife has been even more so. That makes him a good and worthy patron saint for us. We’re even blessed to have a few of his miraculous relics in our own Church!

We’ll celebrate the feast properly on the Weekend and we’ll have a simple Saturday Potluck after the 5:30p Mass for all who are interested.

St. Edward the Confessor, pray for us!
