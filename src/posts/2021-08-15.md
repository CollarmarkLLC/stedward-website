## From The Font

The dogma of the Assumption teaches that at the end of her time on earth, Mary was taken up - body and soul - into heaven. There, she sits at the right hand of Jesus, as Queen of Heaven and Earth. The frequent complaint about this Dogma which has been believed by the Church since the very beginning but which was only codified by extraordinary Papal decree in 1950, is that it’s not explicit in the Bible. The foundation, though, is certainly rooted in Scripture, specifically in Rev 12.

The woman in Rev 12 is identified as Mary, the one “who brought forth a male child, one who is to rule all the nations with a rod of iron... [the one] caught up to God and his throne.” Rev 12 also uses language that reveals the woman to be Daughter Zion, the Queen-Bride of Israel, and the Mother of the Church. Solomon’s bride in the Song of Solomon is similarly described (Songs 6:10). John drives this point home by telling us that the woman wears a crown of twelve stars, an obvious symbol of the twelve tribes of Israel who are also “signs” of the twelve Apostles who, themselves, represent the “new Israel.”

Just as Daughter Zion was a symbol of the chosen people of God—Israel—the woman in Revelation is also a symbol of the new people of God, the Church. Paul, in language quite similar to Revelation, called the Church “the Jerusalem above… our mother.” He also calls the Church the Bride of Christ (Gal 4:26; Eph 5:31–32). Likewise, John referred to the Church as a “Lady” (II Jn:5). The woman of Revelation, however, is more than an allegory for the Church. She is its mother with “offspring” in addition to the one male child to whom she gives birth. And those children are described in Revelation as those who believe in Jesus.

So Mary is Daughter Zion, the Woman who gave birth to the world’s Savior. She is the New Eve and is thus free from the shadow of sin and its consequences which includes the long-term separation of soul and body that the rest of us experience as we wait for the resurrection of the body at the end of time. Mary has been taken up into heaven by God to join her Son in the place He prepared for her. And in that place, as Christ the King’s mother, she sits at His right hand, wearing the crown of the Queen Mother. Other foreshadowings are in the Old Testament in the persons of Enoch and Elijah.

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

Next week, we get back to our Sunday Morning routine of donuts and coffee and a short presentation on Our Faith so that we’re ready to teach our kids and grandkids what they need to know to grow in the knowledge and love of God.

It’s impossible to overstate how important the Faith of the parents and grandparents is to the young. For kids - even kids who have aged to the belief that they don’t need any oversight at all - “normal” is whatever they grow up knowing. My nephews don’t think mask mandates are insane, they’re too young to know how bizarre such a thing is and so they adapt and overcome.

When it comes to Faith, little things like prayer before meals, a family prayer at bedtime, the choice as a family to share some small penance for a very clear reason (like the upcoming day of fasting for those who have suffered or died from COVID that our governor has requested) have a profound effect on the way that kids see the world. I still remember Lent as a little boy and how my mother took it seriously. I remember, too, how my father and my non-Catholic grandparents really didn’t get what Lent was or why we were doing it. Their disinterest had a huge effect upon my own lack of care for religious practice as a child and a teenager. Parents and grandparents in our part of the world have a tremendous impact upon the Faith of the young, far more than we typically realize or acknowledge.

That’s one of the primary reasons that I moved us away from a classroom-based approach to religious instruction and toward a more family-based approach. I know it’s more work for mom and dad and grandma and grandpa. But religious education is far more than dogmas and bible verses… The real lessons that matter - the lessons that will stick through adolescence, college, and the immaturity of youth - are the lessons about priority and practice. If mom and dad go to confession, the kids will go and will keep going. If mom and dad pray before meals, so will the kids - whether they remember the specific prayer or not. If mom and dad face struggle and hardship by visiting the church and praying, so will the kids. If mom and dad go to Church no matter what… If mom and dad take Lent seriously… If mom and dad show generosity to the poor… If mom and dad visit the sick… If mom and dad honor their own fathers and mothers… so will the kids.

By passing the work of religious education back onto you as parents and grandparents, I’m undermining a common and dangerous Catholic attitude that religion belongs at Church and that only the priest really needs to believe and\or live it. Hopefully, parents and\or grandparents are using the books (that we provide free of charge) to go over the short (3-6 pages) weekly lessons which start a dialogue between adult and child. Some families have texted me into the evening to ask an unexpected question that came up on the ride home from Sunday Mass or from the quick review of that Sunday’s lesson. I hope to have that happen more and more as families make religious practice a priority in the home and beyond it! THANK YOU to those who have already done so and THANK YOU to those who intend to do so this year!
