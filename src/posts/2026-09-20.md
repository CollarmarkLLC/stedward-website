## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

Our daily Mass goers know that there are certain mistranslations of the Bible that really get my goat. Today is one of them. The usual daily wage for a laborer was a coin called a Denarius. It's the same coin that Jesus talks about when He asks “whose name and whose face is on this coin?” It's the same coin that Jesus talks about right before He gets into the story of the prodigal son. And it’s a real thing…

A Denarius is “incarnate.” It’s real. It has history. It has shape. It has a name. The “usual daily wage” is an idea. Besides being a clunky phrase, it has no reality to it. The whole Gospel today depends on the idea that everyone who started work early agreed to work the day for one Denarius. And so when the people who worked only one hour got a Denarius - as opposed to a copper coin or some kind of in-kind payment like wheat or bread - the whole-day people assumed they would get a bonus… But the point of the story is that the Denarius symbolizes salvation. And so the bonus doesn’t matter…

But the usual daily wage is such a modern idea that it feels more like a bank doing direct deposit than it does something real and tangible.

This is a good soap box for me to stand on because it illustrates how much the incarnational - the REAL, TANGIBLE, SENSIBLE - images that Jesus uses matter. Modernity LOVES to replace the real with the theoretical. Dr Thomas Sewell says that all of Modernity is replacing that which works with that which sounds good. The teachings of Jesus are not ideas… The Pearl of Great Price that costs us everything is not an idea - it’s a visceral image. The image of the weeds and the wheat works the same way. If we reduced that to the parable of the “poorly managed crops…” Well, it loses more than a turn of phrase.

The same sort of thing happens when we replace the Jewish way of counting hours with the modern o’clock system. This is the gospel that gives us the idea of the “11th hour.” And the idea of the hours is that there is a limit. Five O’clock doesn’t really mean anything to us… When darkness falls, we just turn on a light. Heck, in my house, all the lights are automatic… They change colors around 5pm… It’s neat, but for us the idea of late afternoon doesn’t really mean anything. The idea of the hours passing, though, is a very different kind of image. As the hours tick by, the landowner keeps going out and finding people wasting the day… And when he goes out at the 11th hour, there is a sense of pity and condemnation when he says “Have you been standing around all day?” When they say, “No one has hired us,” we all ask the same question, where were you all day when this guy came to this same place hiring people? The 11th hour is a more visceral and a more incarnate way of thinking about the finite nature of the day. The modern o’clock usage depends on that idea-based way of modern thinking where everything is continuous. Like the Denarius, the original words of Jesus - as opposed to this modern translation - really communicate the finite and scoped and incarnate nature of the story.

I think this story is one of the main reasons that I strongly recommend the RSV (Revised Standard Version) of the Bible. It does a great job of avoiding pitfalls and mistakes like this which have such a larger impact on our understanding than we might immediately realize.

It’s also a strong reminder of our need to think about the Bible properly. On the one hand, the Bible is a powerful tool for prayer. The inspired stories are amazing and uplifting and challenging. At the same time, every Bible we read is a translation… an interpretation of what is being said. Today’s Gospel, for example, is a story about the urgency of serving the Lord and how salvation requires REAL WORK. But with problematic translations like “usual daily wage” and “o’clock”, we can read this same Gospel and come away thinking it’s about being ok with not getting extra pay for extra work… Which is NOT the point of this Gospel.

And so we should be supremely cautious of reading the Bible in English in order to draw doctrinal, moral, or theological conclusions. Rather, we should prioritize using a good translation and praying with scripture over and above studying or analyzing it.
