## From The Font

_“But a Samaritan traveler…”_

Imagine the vitriol and fury that the political left has for someone who isn’t politically correct… Or imagine the disgust that a Vegan might feel at being served rare steak… Or imagine any person who perverts something that you hold dear at the center of your very self-identity…

The Samaritans took a handful of core Jewish beliefs and changed them up… Specifically, they set up their own altars and dismissed the need for making sacrifices in the Temple at Jerusalem. While that seems like a fairly minor problem for us, it was a really, really, really big deal for most of the Jews. The Samaritans were basically heretics who rubbed their error in the face of the “faithful” Jews all the time.

Obviously, Jesus chose the Samaritans for this story because they were the most despicable class of people that the Jews had. Even the Romans and the Persians weren’t so bad as the Samaritans.

Nowadays, we could easily insert into the story a class of people that we find despicable and vile. For me, that might be “Catholics for Choice” - a delusional group who blaspheme the Lord by their very existence. The point isn’t just unpleasant people, but people that evoke a personal sense of disgust - people that evoke a strong, emotional reaction.

And to be clear, this class of people that disgusts us isn’t being judged unfairly because of their ethnicity or their creed, the Samaritans were people whose CHOICES were heretical! Jesus wasn’t condemning unfair prejudge against the Samaritans, the Samaritans were heretics! His message isn’t about acceptance of others, it’s about how we are supposed to treat others…

When we think of our version of the Samaritan who evokes an emotional reaction, we’re not thinking of a loveable scamp… We’re thinking of someone who does bad things and has no intention of stopping or apologizing… Now, can I be neighbor to that person? Can I show mercy to that person? Can I accept help from that person? Can I forgive and love that person even if they don’t forgive and love me?

The Good Samaritan isn’t about overcoming prejudice at all! It’s about loving people who don’t deserve it - which is hard and Christ-like.

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

This Monday is the feast of St Benedict of Nursia. He was the founder of the Benedictine religious order in 529AD in the little town of Subiaco in central Italy.

Benedict was a brilliant young man and his family was able to send him to Rome to study. This was about 100 years after the collapse of the Roman Empire, and the great city was already falling into disrepair. Benedict was disappointed in the city of Rome, especially its religious opportunities. Following the example of St. Basil the Great, he decided to abandon this worldly life and become a hermit. So he gassed up the donkey and rode to the small town of Subiaco where he found a top-notch cave and set about being a hermit. He devoted himself entirely to prayer. The locals brought him a bit of food as they could spare it and many of them made a habit of visiting the hermit for spiritual advice. The idea of a hermit welcoming visitors can seem odd… but it was relatively normal at the time.

As Benedict’s reputation spread, more and more people sought him out as an advisor and some asked to move into his cave or into neighboring caves so as to pursue “the life of perfection” under his instruction. Benedict allowed some and rejected others, but he began work on a “rule of life” which could be adopted by anyone.

The Rule of St. Benedict is now one of the most important religious documents in history. When it was completed, Benedict moved out of the cave and set up a monastery in which he could live alongside the spiritual “novices” who wanted to join him.

When the Subiaco Monastery grew too large, Benedict set up another one in a town called Monte Cassino. It was there that he finished his “Rule for Monks” and it was Monte Cassino that many credit with preserving the Church through the so-called “Dark Ages.”

The Benedictines were unquestionably the most important religious order in the Church from their founding until the spread of the “mendicant” orders, especially the Franciscans and the Dominicans. They preserved religious and secular writings and books. They codified music and art. They really are an amazing order.

The Benedictines were still growing and thriving until their sudden and precipitous collapse following Vatican II. In the last 60 years, the number of monasteries has fallen dramatically and the order has few religious novices. As with so many great religious orders, a few higher-up embraced liberal theology and the whole order is now dying a tragic death.

Still, I was privileged to study at a Benedictine Monastery! The monks were crazy! No joke, they were certifiable. And yet the experience of monastic prayer and the monastic life - even when it’s being led by insane people - is powerful and evangelical. There are a handful of new Monasteries using only the old Latin Mass that are thriving and I’m hopeful for the future of the Order under their prayerful leadership.

PRAYER FOR HURRICANE SEASON… O God, Master of this passing world, hear the humble voices of your children. The Sea of Galilee obeyed your order and returned to its former quietude; you are still the Master of land and sea. We live in the shadow of a danger over which we have no control. The Gulf, like a provoked and angry giant, can awake from its seeming lethargy, overstep its conventional boundaries, invade our land and spread chaos and disaster. During this hurricane season, we turn to You, O loving Father. Spare us from past tragedies whose memories are still so vivid and whose wounds seem to refuse to heal with the passing of time. O Virgin, Star of the Sea, Our Beloved Mother, we ask you to plead with your Son in our behalf so that spared from the calamities common to this area and animated with a true spirit of gratitude, we will walk in the footsteps of your Divine Son to reach the heavenly Jerusalem where a stormless eternity awaits us. Amen (Fr. Al Volpe, Cameron Parish, LA, originally dedicated to the victims of Hurricane Audrey in 1957)
