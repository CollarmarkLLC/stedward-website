## From The Font

_He stood up to read and was handed a scroll of the prophet Isaiah…_

The Book of Isaiah straddles an horrific moment in Jewish history. In about 600 BC, the Jews were defeated in battle by the Babylonian Empire and almost the entire population was forcibly relocated to modern-day Iran. The temple was destroyed and the holy objects were desecrated. It was the unimaginable worst-case scenario. For many Jews, it was as close to “God is Dead” as can be imagined…

The Jews remained in Babylon as second class citizens for about 60 years. Miraculously, the Jewish religion persisted through the Prophets like Jeremiah and Isaiah and the leadership of Priest\Governors like Ezra and Nehemiah. Understandably, the writings of these people are deeply sad and unrelentingly critical of the sins and faults of the people who picked a fight with the Babylonians in the first place. But, as I said, after about 60 years, it suddenly and unexpectedly ended.

The Book of Isaiah isn’t actually a book by a guy named Isaiah. It’s more like a collection of sermons from a group founded by the Prophet Isaiah. The first two thirds of the book come from the hard days of the Exile. They are dire and hard hitting and only occasionally hopeful. But the last chunk comes from the time when the return to Israel had begun.

This last section was written while the Jews were shocked to be set free from their captivity. In fact, they were given back a lot of the sacred items for the temple. They were even given cash to rebuild the temple. And so they went from thinking God was basically dead to being flabbergasted that God wasn’t dead. In fact, they had just been punished by Him and now, He was ready to start blessing them again.

SO! When Jesus reads these words just as the Jews have resigned themselves to being conquered by the Romans… Is it any surprise that folks lost their cool? It wasn’t really the spiritual component that most of the people listening understood, it was the politics.

For us now, it should be the spiritual aspect that we consider. But the historical context is important all the same.

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

This Friday is the feast day of Thomas Aquinas who is the patron of Students and Teachers. He was an Italian born in the 1200s to a rich family. He was also brilliant and his family had a big plan for him. Much to their frustration, Thomas joined the Dominicans - an order which took a vow of poverty - and left his family’s money and power to study in Paris at the most important university in the world at the time.

In Paris, he studied under a saint named Albert and became a master of Philosophy. Almost immediately, he got into hot water with some of the higher-ups because of his genius. Thomas was unafraid to read anything, including the recently republished writings of Aristotle, the greatest student of Plato.

At the time, lots and lots of books and topics were simply off-limits. Thomas, simply, didn’t care. He read everything. For a while, he was blacklisted as a speaker and teacher. But he never backed down from a challenge and he considered preaching and writing to be as important as teaching. He wrote hundreds of historically important books, documents, commentaries, and essays. He also wrote the music and prayers for the Feast of Corpus Christi and the hymns that we sing now for Adoration.

Despite his brilliance, Thomas was a deeply prayerful man and toward the end of his life, he started to resist writing in favor of praying. But his spiritual director and the head honcho of his Monastery (AKA the Prior) demanded - through Holy Obedience - that he continue to write. In the midst of finishing his most important work, the Summa Theologica, Thomas was so overcome with a vision in prayer that he rushed upstairs to his study intending to burn the entire book. His young secretary, also a Dominican brother, rushed into the fire and saved the book from destruction. There are some wonderful legends about that young man… At any rate, Thomas was also forbidden by the Prior and his spiritual director from damaging his writings in the future.

St. Thomas Aquinas is the single most important philosopher of the middle ages and he’s probably in the top five most important philosophers in world history alongside Plato, Aristotle, Confucius and St. Augustine. It really is impossible to overstate the importance of St. Thomas.

His life is a pretty good example for any student who is serious about learning anything - whether it’s algebra, religion, piano or pinball… Learning demands a certain degree of natural aptitude. Then it demands study and practice. It also demands that we have our broader life and our human nature in good working order. From there, we need coaches we can trust and we need passion. It may be hard to put all that together for a subject in school, but we as human beings must be life-long learners. If we content ourselves with what we already know, we will quickly begin to decline and to lose whatever we have. But if we’re constantly trying to learn a little more and to teach what we have learned to others, then we’ll be more fully human, more intellectually acute, and much more personally happy. That goes for everything from minor stuff like languages and hobbies to essential things like the Lord Jesus.
