## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

As with the feast of the Assumption on Saturday, Jesus in today’s Gospel draws the line between the natural and the supernatural person within each of us. In the natural sense, this Canaanite woman wants that to which she is not entitled - namely the healing of Jesus. This is exactly like the scripture for the Assumption when the Virgin Mary appears and wants to speak with Jesus. In both cases, Jesus gives them a hard time in the natural sense. He says, “who are my mother and my brothers?” Here, He reminds her twice that the Canaanites are heretics.

In both cases, though, Jesus ends the story by moving beyond the natural to the supernatural. On the assumption, Jesus says, “Rather, Blessed are those who hear the word of God and Keep it.” Here, He says, “O woman, Great is your faith! Let it be done as you wish.”

As Christians, we’re not free to remain on the purely natural level. Christ commands us to interact with one another on both the natural and the supernatural level. That’s how something like hospitality can be considered a truly Christian virtue!

On the natural level, we are called to the Corporal Works of Mercy: To feed the hungry; To give drink to the thirsty; To clothe the naked; To harbor the harborless; To visit the sick; To ransom the captive; and To bury the dead.

We can do this by giving to organizations dedicated to these works of mercy. We can do it by helping those people we encounter in the course of life. We can do it when God gives us the opportunity.

We have to be careful NOT to dismiss these worldly works as unimportant. Remember that in Matthew’s Gospel, Jesus says that judgment for heaven is based upon when we see “Him” hungry and gave him food, thirsty and gave him drink, in prison and visited him…

But neither can we feel like giving $100 to some charity covers our bases. Beyond the corporal works of mercy (ordered to the natural person), we have the spiritual works of mercy (ordered to the supernatural person in each of us).

The Spiritual Works of Mercy are: To instruct the ignorant; To counsel the doubtful; To admonish sinners; To bear wrongs patiently; To forgive offenses willingly; To comfort the afflicted; To pray for the living and the dead.

These are the hard ones. It’s easy enough to pray for the living and the dead. It’s not too hard to comfort the afflicted. But it takes real humility to bear wrongs patiently and to forgive offenses willingly. It may also take some study and meditation to instruct the ignorant and to counsel the doubtful.

But the one that takes real courage nowadays is to admonish sinners. It’s hard to call someone out on their sin and to find a way to convince them to leave that sin behind. It’s hard because our world has a faulty notion of judgement. It’s hard because our world has convinced itself that truth, salvation, and real love don’t matter. It’s hard because we value being accepted and being perceived as broad-minded and avoiding conflict too much.

In fact, we value being accepted, being broad-minded and avoiding conflict even over love! Authentic love desires the good - both natural and supernatural - of the other even more than the relationship itself.

What’s more - no one should ever merely be broad-mindedly “tolerated.” No one should ever be reduced to the group of which they are a part. No one should ever be dismissed as not being “worth” my suffering for their good.

Jesus isn’t vague on sin. He’s very clear and nothing has changed since that fateful Good Friday 2000 years ago. Sin leads to damnation. Period. The Church freely forgives sin for the repentant. And the Church charges all of us - clergy and laity - to admonish the sinner that they might discover the freedom of Jesus Christ.

I have no right to cast personal judgements upon the natural person. None of us do! But I have a Christian duty and an obligation in love to share the teachings of Jesus Christ (the easy ones and the hard ones) with every single person I love.
