## From The Font

_“Who do people say that I am?”_

On the surface, this famous question would seem to be superficial and to reveal something akin to immaturity in Jesus… Until we recall that Jesus is always concerned with revealing His true Divine nature and with revealing the Father to us. And so when Jesus asks who the people say that He is, He is doing so to help the Apostles come to a realization.

He wants them to call to mind their own guesses and assumptions as well as the thoughts and ideas of the people they’ve encountered along the way over the past few years.

The Apostles are shockingly short of things to say. They mention some of the Prophets, official and unofficial, but they don’t have anything groundbreaking or anything that reveals deep consideration or deep prayer. It’s only Peter who steps out of the crowd with an original thought. After much prayer and consideration, Peter is able to identify Jesus as the Christ. Immediately, Jesus praises Peter and reminds the entire college of Apostles that Jesus’ Divine Nature has been revealed to them by the Father…

This event surely parallels the climax of the Bread of Life Narrative in which Jesus reminds the crowd twice that they can only come to believe in His Eucharistic teaching if the Father grants it to them and then Peter concludes with his admission: “Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of Everlasting Life!”

Whereas we usually ask questions in order to gain knowledge, Jesus already knows all there is to know. So when Jesus asks a question in Scripture, we need to give it special attention… We want to ask 1) what the question is, 2) what the answer is, 3) who is being asked, 4) what the context around the question is, 5) what similar questions are posed by Jesus elsewhere in Scripture, and 6) why - to the best of our understanding - is Jesus asking the question. There’s great depth in some of the simplest questions in Scripture. For practice, apply these six queries to Jesus’ protest of Mary in John’s story of the Wedding Feast at Cana: _Woman, what concern is that of me?!?_

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

In the Church, each month has a featured devotion and September’s featured devotion is Our Lady of Sorrows, often called “Mater Dolorosa” after the Latin title. The feast follows the day after the Exaltation of the Holy Cross which was put on the Calendar to honor and celebrate the Cross of Christ and to commemorate the finding of the true cross by St. Helena in the 4th century.

The feast of Our Lady of Sorrows commemorates the Way of the Cross from the Blessed Virgin Mary’s point of view. While Jesus suffered unjustly judged, being scourged, carrying the Cross, and ultimately dying upon it, His poor mother experienced sadness that few of us could comprehend.

Of course, Mary was warned to ready herself for this all the way back at Jesus’ presentation in the Temple. After his circumcision, Jesus, Mary, and Joseph encountered two prophets on their way out of the temple: Simeon and Anna. Simeon prophesied about Jesus’ future works and then, in an easy to overlook line, he turns to Mary and says “a sword will pierce your heart as well.”

Christianity is a religion for the worship of Jesus Christ. Jesus tells us that a human being who loves God rightly will naturally - without any other effort or intent - love his neighbor and, in fact, his enemy. The right worship of God, then, includes a certain veneration (not worship…) of those whose lives show forth the glory of God. We, for example, naturally venerate someone like Mother Theresa because she was so selfless and holy and the love of God just bubbled up out of her...

The same logic applies to the veneration of Mary. Her sorrow doesn’t even begin to compare to the suffering of Jesus, but that doesn’t mean her sorrow doesn’t move us!

The Feast of Our Lady of Sorrows is a potent reminder that there is an essential role for suffering in the Christian life. Our modern world worships the golden calf of positivity and tells us that we must avoid suffering at all costs no matter what. Our world tells us that all death, all sorrow, all negativity, etc, is simply bad. But our religion is the worship of a God-man who explicitly chose to suffer and to die and Who instructs us to do the same: “Unless you take up your cross and follow me, you cannot inherit eternal life.”

Friends, suffering is not bad or evil in and of itself. We praise suffering in athletes and in students and in soldiers and in the self-sacrifice of good parents. We despise those who selfishly refuse to suffer along with us by, for example, patiently waiting in line or acting with good manners in public. Many of us invest good money to sit in the frigid cold of a duck blind or to lie under the punishing sun on a sandy beach. We know that some suffering has value and worth.

The Feast of Our Lady of Sorrows reminds us that there are other, far more important, kinds of suffering that deserve even greater respect, appreciation, and attention from us. Her sorrows and her sufferings were intended by God from the moment she said yes to the Holy Spirit at the Annunciation. That only seems bad if we forget that it is all as naught compared to the glory she experiences now in Heaven.
