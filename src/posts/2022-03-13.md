## From The Font

_“Jesus took Peter, John, and James and went up the mountain to pray.”_

Our modern world has made an idol of equality. In the face of obvious and unquestionable evidence that some are more talented, smarter, prettier, and\or whatever than others, modern people tend to take great umbrance that some are treated differently or have greater success than others… Even Christians seem to have convinced themselves that Jesus is opposed to difference and distinction...

But, the truth is that Jesus, Himself, withholds certain teachings for only His apostles. And even with the Apostles, Peter clearly has first position and James and John seem to get preferential treatment and access on a regular basis… And there’s nothing wrong with any of that! Jesus is clear that He has chosen certain people for reasons that He doesn’t make clear. Later on, He will instruct the apostles to choose others to carry on their work by the means of laying on of hands - which sets the method and precedent for the Rite of Ordination.

A thorough look at the Bible will provide very little evidence or support for the modern cult of equality. Surely, Jesus teaches that God loves all and St. Paul adds that within the Church, there is neither Jew nor Greek, etc. Certainly, the Church has always taught that the spiritual treasures of the Church should be given freely regardless of race, gender, wealth, etc.

And yet, some individuals are chosen especially… The Church is established as a hierarchy (for better or worse) with a clear law, a clear sense of governance and authority, and a top-down structure to implement it.

And while modern people tend to criticize and even fight against that structure, it comes directly from Jesus Himself who subdivided His own followers into those who merely listen, those who follow Him (“disciples”), and those to whom He intended to give direct authority (“Apostles”).

There’s always a grave danger in imposing modern ideals (or idols) onto the Christian way of thinking. Human Nature never changes and so the Lord Jesus, Who made us, was able to establish the Church in a lasting way that doesn’t need correction from whatever philosophy happens to be in vogue just now.

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

Well this week we have two of our favorite Saints’ Feast Days: St. Patrick and St. Joseph!

St. Patrick was a courageous bishop who was kidnapped as a young man and who, after his rescue, became a priest and then a bishop and then returned to the homeland of his kidnappers to convert the whole island that we now call Ireland… He was a brilliant writer and preacher. He was a spiritual master. But he’s now known mostly as the patron saint of Beer…

The Irish remain deeply religious to this day. And when the Church said such and such a day was a feast day, the Irish took it seriously. Most Catholics did prior to the last century or so. The feast day of the patron saint of the nation was a BIG DEAL and so the Irish partied in honor of their patron. When the Irish immigrants settled in the US, their rather judgmental neighbors, who disliked them as poor and unlearned, found common ground at the pub. And so when the Irish immigrants in Boston, New Orleans, New York and Philadelphia showed their drinking prowess in honor of St. Patrick, their stock rose with the locals. It was the shared experience of Irish immigrants and local non-immigrants who really made St. Pat’s what it is today. And in an odd, roundabout sort-of way, it was the over-the-top nature of St. Pat’s that began to bridge the gap and set the stage for locals to fully accept those broadly despised immigrant communities. It’s just like St. Patrick to turn something seemingly all bad (like kidnapping and drunkenness) into something good…

St. Joseph, too, opened the door for the acceptance of broadly disliked Italian immigrants to find a welcome in the US. And like St. Patrick, St. Joseph’s feast day really caught on when the locals realized that the Italians could cook and drink with the best of them… And like with St. Patrick, New Orleans was one of the epicenters for the uniquely Italian-American Immigrant celebration that we call the St. Joseph Altar.

It was mostly Sicilians who settled into New Orleans after a great famine in the Fava bean crop over several seasons in the late 1800s. Sicilian families abandoned their island and asked St. Joseph to protect them as they relocated their families in the US. Many promised St. Joseph special devotion if he did so. When they arrived, they celebrated his feast day with a public celebration that involved food, drink and sweets alongside several wonderfully bizarre Italian superstitions and a special concern for fundraising for the poor. The tradition picked up steam as New Orleans Catholics began to participate. The Italian immigrants and the Irish immigrants struggled to play nice, but the Catholic community in New Orleans (with its own strong tradition of religious festivals (Carnivale and Mardi Gras) was happy to eat and drink with anyone. The St. Joseph Altar is still a hugely important and very religious celebration in and around New Orleans.

It’s remarkable to me that famines pushed such wonderful cultural and religious traditions into our history. God really is good all the time!
