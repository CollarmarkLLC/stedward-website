## From The Font

_“Then Peter said, ‘Lord, is this parable meant for us or for everyone?’”_

Most of our local Protestant churches reject the idea of “hierarchy” in discipleship and ministry. That means that they don’t accept the idea that certain disciples get privileged information from the Lord because of their rank, assignment, or whatever.

Today’s Gospel, though, gives us a very clear example that Jesus did restrict certain teachings to His apostles alone. There are other examples such as the Weeds and Wheat or the Sower and Seed both in Mt 13.

The reasons for the Lord’s choice to restrict some teachings are undoubtedly complex! But we can certainly see some of the more obvious aspects of His reasoning. For one thing, the Apostles were being trained to teach as Jesus taught. When one of His sermons used a more complicated image, especially one which Jesus explained only superficially to the crowds, the future teachers needed to understand more details.

For another thing, some of the teachings of Jesus were a bit too intense for the crowds. We see in John 6 how the Eucharistic sermons of Jesus were hard for the people to absorb. So too, were the messages about taking up one’s cross. Jesus could hardly explain to the crowds that He would be crucified by the Sanhedrin in the coming months or years! So some of the teachings of Jesus had to be reserved.

It’s important to be clear that the Gospels were written by the Apostles who were present at the sermons they’re recounting. As authors, these Apostles left out LOTS of details. Jesus’ sermons would’ve lasted for hours and yet we have only a few selected quotations and stories. That’s not a deception or a fault - it’s the reality of ancient writing and bookmaking.

More than anything, that should reaffirm for us just how important the establishment of the formal Church with the Apostles at the top really was! They were the eyewitnesses and they were charged with passing on the message no book could contain!

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

This Wednesday is my favorite patronal feast day in our Church! St. Lawrence was one of the seven deacons of the city of Rome at the time of Pope Sixtus II (~258AD) who were martyred in the persecution of the Christians by the Roman Emperor Valerian.

Lawrence’s martyrdom was especially brutal. He was tortured by being placed on a grill above hot coals. As the hours went by, the pain was certainly excruciating, but Lawrence took the opportunity to bring some humor to the scene by loudly declaring to the Emperor’s soldiers that he was done on this side and that they could turn him over… While it gives us a chuckle, it was profound evidence to the onlookers of the power of Jesus to allow His disciples to overcome even the most horrific bodily and mental torments.

So much of the power of martyrdom comes from just this realization. Just as onlookers might see a Christian expressing great joy or love or peace and find in that something for which they hunger, so, too, onlookers see in a Christian enduring suffering with poise and supernatural courage a reason to consider seeking Faith in Jesus who can give His followers that kind of strength.

The story of St. Lawrence closely parallels the story of the young men in the book of Daniel (ch 3), where Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego are cast into Nebuchadnezzar’s white-hot furnace as a means of execution… But the young men are found to be dancing in the flames accompanied by an angel. The result of the whole event is that the king is moved toward Faith in the God of Israel.

As the young men in Daniel were punished for refusing to bow down to the golden statue of the king, so Lawrence was executed for refusing to burn incense in worship of the emperor cult.

Over time, Lawrence’s humorous quip made him a patron of cooks, especially those who worked around open-fire grills. And so, nowadays, we call Lawrence the patron saint of Barbeque… And in a previous parish, I was able to host an annual barbeque for the deacons of the diocese on the Feast of St. Lawrence. It was a hoot!

These sometimes bizarre patronal saints aren’t just there for fun or quirks. Neither is there some official chart. Surely a farming saint like Isidore the worker is logically a patron of farmers. But a farmer could certainly ask for the intercession of St. Joseph the carpenter or St. Walstan (a farmer and miracle-worker). They could also invoke any saint to whom they feel a connection for success in their life and work.

Even beyond this, we have some saints like Joseph of Cupertino who had a miraculous gift of flight and who is the patron of pilots. We have St. Clair of Assisi who had a miraculous experience of a “window of light” appear next to her through which she could see the Mass being offered in the chapel and so she is the patroness of TV… These are humorous to be sure, but they also invite us to recall the Lord’s constant involvement in every aspect of life, not just the Church part.

Why not ask for prayer from St. Lawrence as we light the pit and from St. Andrew, patron of cattleman as we cook up a delicious steak?
