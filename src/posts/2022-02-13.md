## From The Font

_“Jesus came down with the Twelve and stood on a stretch of level ground.”_

It’s hard to overstate how much effect speakers and microphones have had on preaching and teaching. Before amplification, any speech given to a group of more than fifteen or twenty needed a specially-designed room or space. A trained speaker or actor can be heard by a thousand or so people from a raised dais or in a Greek theater. That’s part of the reason that most of the sermons Jesus gave were given in the temple (where there were plenty of raised rostra from which to speak) or on a mountain side (where the people could arrange themselves like a Greek theater).

For the most famous part of His most famous sermon, Jesus deliberately walks down from the mount (from which the “Sermon on the Mount” gets its name) and stands on level ground with His listeners. While it would have been much harder to hear, the symbolism would have made it worth it. The so-called Beatitudes (a name taken from the Latin word for “Blessed are you”) are spoken not from above but from a place of shared rank. It’s not quite about equality (which is a VERY modern and deeply problematic idea) but rather about God descending among us in order to raise us up beyond where we could raise ourselves.

In just the same way that an adult has to bend down to talk face to face with a toddler, so the Lord must descend from Heaven to speak with us. And just as that adult must bend down in order to pick up the toddler, so God descends among us to lift us up to a higher place than we could achieve on our own.

Now, sharp readers will note that the Gospel today isn’t actually the “official” Beatitudes, those are found in Matthew’s recounting of this sermon… Good catch! Both Matthew’s version (Mt 5-7) and Luke’s tell about the same sermon, they just choose different sections of it to record in their Gospels.

Nowadays, we tend to think of sermons as 10-20 minute speeches with a structure and, hopefully, a clear starting and stopping point. But for Jesus, sermons could go on for hours and include meals and Q&A and a few healings and a few venue changes. Oh, how times have changed…

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

Well, happy Valentine’s day everyone!

There are a slew of Catholic religious feasts which, for various historical reasons, became entirely non-religious public celebrations. Christmas is perhaps the biggest one. The English revival of Christmas in the so-called Dickensian period wasn’t about religious fervor, it was more about public morale and a need for patriotic nostalgia.

It’s not necessarily a bad thing. Festivals are an important part of being human! That’s why they’re part of our religious practice. Sure, it can be complicated or even detrimental when some of us are keeping the religious feast of Christmas, some of us are keeping it non-religiously and most of us are trying to keep it both religiously and non-religiously. Even so, it helps to understand the human need for feast, for shared celebration, and for setting aside specific days for specific human celebrations.

Valentine’s Day is a celebration of romantic love. And gosh is that important to celebrate! Yes, there’s some sadness that we’re ignoring the brutal beheading of a Bishop in the 3rd century. It’s sad that the patronal feast of Beekeepers is overshadowed by candy hearts and chocolate. But there’s a lot of good that can come from celebrating Valentine’s day non-religiously.

But let’s clear up one thing that can derail the whole shebang: the Celebration of romantic love is not a celebration of lust. Even in its non- religious context, Valentine’s day is meant to be a celebration of romance. It’s meant to be a day when all of us - from the single folks to the long-married folks - can appreciate and even celebrate the wonders of human romance. From the butterflies in the stomach to the euphoria of the first kiss to the optimism and hopefulness of could this be “the one” through to the joys and passions of the honeymoon and beyond, human romance is indescribably amazing. And while it changes over the years of marriage, enthusiastic spouses can and should use their deepened understanding-of and love-for their spouses to continue those romantic gestures which they know their love will appreciate and enjoy.

Genuine love grows and evolves and deepens. Mutual appreciation and friendship unite the spouses with a shared sense of life’s value, worth, and purpose. While it will look different for every couple and it’s neither automatic or easy, the years of marriage can and should be filled with romantic human love. Even cultures which vigorously dislike public displays of affection (e.g. the Japanese or the Russians) joyfully celebrate the romantic gestures of couples celebrating monumental anniversaries of marriage.

Marriage is, after all, both a human and a divine institution. Marriage is there at the beginning of the Bible. Jesus’s first miracle is at a wedding where He ensures the abundance of human joy for the revelers. Even the Book of Revelation ends with a description of Heaven, itself, as an eternal wedding feast.

Romantic love is a good thing! So long as we’re careful that it doesn’t descend into sinful lust, Valentine’s Day should absolutely be a cause for celebration!
