## From The Font

_“I urge you to live in a manner worthy of the call you have received.”_

In Christianity and in humanity in general, one size does not fit all. Some of us are brilliant thinkers and so we have a duty to put our minds at the Lord’s service. Others of us are artists or organizers or wealthy or whatever else and we have the duty to put those blessings at God’s service.

While we all share this fundamental duty toward the Lord in Justice, the “manner worthy” of one of us fulfilling that duty is going to look dramatically different than it would for another of us!

At times, that seems grossly unfair. To whom much is given, much is expected… And so one person can please God mightily with something as simple as making himself available to talk with people. St. Andre Bessette, for example, was a simple man of great faith. He has no great intellect or talents that the Monastery down the road needed, but he was able to be the gatekeeper. He set up a chair on the front porch of the gatehouse and after several years, the line to sit on the porch with this ‘simple’ man was hours long. He built one of the most beautiful Churches in the world by being faithful to sitting on the porch and visiting…

Compare him to one of the great minds of the 20th century - Pope St. John Paul II. John Paul’s ‘worth manner’ meant being hit by a truck, shot, serving as the second-longest ruling pope in history, writing thousands of pages of esoteric philosophy, and dying in the public spotlight. The outline of his life is pages and pages long.

Each of these two saints lived in a manner worthy of the call they received, but their lives couldn’t be more different.

So too, we mustn't waste time comparing our own lives and duties and service of God to another elses. We can certainly take as an example the lives of the Holy men and women in the Tradition of the Church and in the people we know - but those examples only serve to assist us, not to define us. The urging of St. Paul is to live in a manner worthy of the call that YOU have received, not in a manner consistent with the call others have received.

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

By now, many of you will have read about the sudden and largely unexpected decision of Pope Francis to limit the celebration of the Traditional Latin Mass. While the Holy Father claims to have done so in the interest of bringing unity to the Church, the immediate reaction around the world has been almost universally divisive. Papal documents are almost always drafted by a panel of experts rather than the Pope himself. That’s been true for the last several centuries. As such, we ought not to ascribe to the Holy Father directly whatever flaws in tone any document bearing his name might contain. Sadly, this document, titled Traditiones Custodes, is quite difficult to read. It tends to imply that all Catholics who express an interest in the traditional aspects of our Faith do so as an act of division or pride.

I’ve written my own thoughts on Facebook and have shared with a few specific parishioners more completely my concerns and the genuine sense of heartbreak and abandonment that I feel from His Holiness in this promulgation.

In my article in the bulletin last week, as a matter of complete coincidence, I wrote about the specific limitations of the Papal “charism” of Infallibility as last weeks’ bulletin was published on the anniversary of the publication of Vatican I.

I’d like to briefly extend my thoughts from that article in light of the events of this past week…

What do we do if we disagree with the Pope?

I’m not talking about Pope Francis preferring Pagliacci to Bach. I’m not talking about Pope John Paul’s preferences for modern art over the renaissance painters. I’m not talking about Pope Paul VI or Pope Benedict preferring to choose professors as bishops over canon lawyers or pastors. I’m asking what we do if the Pope says something as a legal act (as he has done in this de facto ban on the Old Mass) and we believe that he is in the wrong?

Well, there’s a lot to say here about the duty we have to respect our Pope, our Bishop and our Pastor. There’s much to say, too, about the role of obedience in the spiritual life. I’ve written and preached on these topics and, no doubt, they’ll come up again. The short answer to the short question is that disagreeing with the Pope is not that big of a deal.

The Pope has a limited purview in a certain and specific sphere of ideas. If I disagree with the Pope on a matter of, say, economics or medicine. So what?! Provided I express my disagreement with respect, there’s nothing wrong at all with that. In matters well outside the realm of religion, theology, morality and Church governance, the Pope is no more qualified to speak than is any bishop, priest, or well-meaning Christian. Provided that we disagree with respect and courtesy, we are free to express ourselves. That’s even more the case if we have specific expertise in the matters being discussed, eg epidemiology.

When our disagreement is within the realm of religion, we need to be more cautious, more deferential, and more prayerful. Most of what the Pope writes is not “infallible,” but it does come from the Chair of Peter and that calls for humility, prayerful discernment, and public restraint.
