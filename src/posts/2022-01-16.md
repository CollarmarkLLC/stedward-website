## From The Font

_“...there were six stone water jars there… each holding twenty to thirty gallons…”_

It takes six ordinary bottles of wine to make one gallon, so Jesus made about nine hundred bottles of wine for this wedding party… There are LSU fraternities that couldn’t get through that in a month. So what gives?

Well, there are three factors. First, Jewish weddings were five or six (or more) day-long parties back-to-back rather than the rehearsal supper plus reception that we have. People went home to sleep and came back. They went to work and came back. A wealthy family could end up feeding most of the village for a week. An average family wouldn’t be able to foot the bill for everything, and so regular folks would just bring their own food and booze. Free food or not, a party was a party.

Second, Jewish life was hard. Farming and fishing in the desert is a tough gig. When an opportunity to find some joy in the drudgery of life arose - whether it was a religious feast or a family celebration - they took it seriously and lived the good life for a little while.

Third, wine had strong religious connotations. Unlike ordinary food, wine “cheered the heart” and had a sort of mystical effect on everyone. Certainly, people understood what alcohol was and what it did. There were other distilled spirits as well in the ancient world… But wine was seen as a gift from the Lord that lifted people out of themselves for the moment and gave them a euphoric sense of joy. And while the habit of drunkenness was condemned by Jesus more than once, the fact that He began His ministry by making A LOT of wine has powerful symbolism in the ancient mind.

Most of us reading this are, by comparison, unthinkably wealthy. We rarely go whole days without eating and even hot and cold weather don’t really affect us. We have “first world problems.” Even death is mitigated by our Christian Faith. For people facing threats like famine, starvation, and death without any sense of eternity, the miracle of the Water and Wine pointed to a God who actually cared about His people. He didn’t just care about getting His worship and sacrifices… He cares about their lives and their joy. It’s a powerful message!

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

Calendars aren’t exactly exciting stuff! But I marvel at how different our religious calendar looked like only sixty years ago…

So Advent and Christmas were like they are now. When Epiphany was celebrated on the 12th day of Christmas (not some random Sunday), the Christmas Season ended. That didn’t begin the season of “ordinary time” because “ordinary time” is a terrible name for anything! Traditionally, the time after Epiphany was called… the “Time After Epiphany” because spiritually and religiously, the idea was to reflect upon that specific Christmas Season and apply those lessons to our lives. That’s something we should still be doing.

Now, depending on how long the time between Epiphany and Ash Wednesday was that year and depending upon where you lived, the overlapping season of Carivale (literally: “farewell to meat”) might begin right away or it may be only the last week or two before Lent began.

Religiously, there was actually a whole extra season of the Church year called “Septuagesima” (literally “Seventy Days”) that functioned as a kind of pre-Lent. At Mass, it was like Lent was already happening, but outside of Mass, the fasting and other Lent stuff didn’t start until Ash Wednesday.

Lent itself had an unofficial three-part structure. You had Lent, “Passiontide,” and “Triduum.” (literally, “three days”). Because the Lenten season required real fasting (not just giving up coke or chocolate) every day except Sunday, the arrival of Rose Sunday was an exciting sign that we were well fast halfway. The statues were covered for Passiontide. And Holy Week, of course, ended with the Triduum of Holy Thursday, Good Friday, and Holy Saturday. After nine weeks of Lent at Mass and 40 days of fasting on a single meat-free meal each day, Easter was a serious time of rejoicing and fasting. It certainly helps us to get in the headspace of today’s Gospel where Jesus multiplies the wine for a party.

The Easter season began with celebration. After forty days, on a Thursday and not a Sunday, we celebrated the Ascension. Then, ten days later, the Pentecost. Like with Epiphany, the time after Pentecost wasn’t “ordinary” but directed toward the spiritual lessons that we had learned in the forty days of Lent and the fifty days of Easter.

The old calendar made a lot of sense from a human perspective. The new one is most antiseptic and bland and tends to prioritize a minimum of effort for everyone - clergy and laity alike. That’s not a very good thing for anyone.

For us here in Louisiana, where so much of our culture and history is deep in Catholicism and where that history and culture should be a benefit when it comes to making converts, it’s sad that the Church has thrown out much of her own history. But there’s a good lesson in that, too.

Simply put, nothing exists without context. Something wonderful like Mardi Gras or the Wedding Feast and Cana - if it becomes divorced from its context, will lose all of its meaning and spiritual value and ultimately become meaningless or even destructive. It’s up to us to remember and re-teach the context and meaning of the things we care about and love!
