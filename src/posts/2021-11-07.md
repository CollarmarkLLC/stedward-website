## From The Font

_“...a widow was gathering sticks there...”_

Widows and widowers have always had a hard time of it… In Christianity, there’s lots to know and understand about the vocation of marriage, but too little is said about how widows and widowers are to embrace “post-vocational” life…

In the ancient world, the general plan was that when the kids had moved out of the house - which typically happened closer to 15 years of age than to 20 - the empty nest couple would take on a roll of moral leadership in the extended family and continue to lead their lives as before. When one of the couple died, the other would move in with one of the children.

When Jesus showed up on the scene, the Jewish people had already been in an off-and-on war with the Roman Empire for three generations… The supply of healthy young men was low and the supply of unmarried women was high… Roman taxes and war retribution also made it all the more difficult for families to absorb their widowed parents.

The image of a lonely widow - especially one taking care of her apparently layabout son - is in a word sad… The widow of Zarephath is facing what many Americans are facing right now: a poorly managed economy, poverty, and a lack of available food at any price. Even with needs as simply as flour and oil, she can’t make ends meet.

When we meet Elijah in this story, he had just finished being the mouthpiece of God Who has just sent this drought-based famine on the world. Elijah meets the widow and he sees the effects of his prophecy. Through Elijah, God asks the poor, dying woman for even more… She is asked to give a portion of her last meal to a stranger. When she freely does, all her needs are provided for!

When Jesus points out the same behavior in a poor widow who gives from her “want” (her poverty) rather than her surplus, He calls His disciples to note the attitude. Soon, He will expect them to lay down their lives for Him…

What is expected from each of us will be different, but the attitude the Lord calls for from us is just the same as the poor widow of Zarephath.

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

This Saturday is the feast of “Mother Cabrini” (St. Francis Cabrini, MSC) born in Italy in 1850 and foundress (in 1880) of the ​​Missionary Sisters of the Sacred Heart of Jesus. She brought her new order to the United States in 1887 and went on to become the first US Citizen to be canonized as a saint!

But that’s not why I’m writing about here in beautiful Tallulah… no, I’m remembering her in our bulletin because as a very young boy living in New Orleans, our beloved Charles Paschal Greco met Mother Cabrini. In fact, he met her twice. She encouraged him and told him that he would be something special if he dedicated himself to the Lord’s service.

Of course, Charlie Greco went on to become Fr. Greco and then Monsignor Greco and then the guy-who-actually-did-all-the-Archbishop’s-work Greco and finally, our Bishop, Charles P. Greco, Bishop of Alexandria.

Bishop Greco is, of course, fondly remembered by many of us for his winning personality, his gentlemanly charm, and his sincere love for the Lord and for the autistic children of St. Mary’s in Alexandria and of Our Lady’s in Shreveport. And while I think Charlie Greco would have become Bishop Greco whether or not he had met Mother Cabrini, the fact that he pushed so hard to have a hospital, an orphanage, and a parish named after her gives testimony to the impact that a single encounter with a saint can have. It also speaks to how much a word of encouragement can mean to a young person.

When I was a senior in High School, I attended daily Mass in the afternoon at 5:30p. One little old lady, in particular, never failed to talk to me after Mass and to encourage me to consider becoming a priest… Mrs. Liz Faulk is as close to a saint as I’m likely to meet. I don’t know how my life would have been different if I had met her when I was 9 years old (as Bishop Greco did with Mother Cabrini), but I know the impact she had when I was 17.

The lesson I drew from Mrs Faulk and from the story that Bishop Greco told about Mother Cabrini was simply to encourage young people to think beyond what the world offers. And, really, that’s not limited to young people! Any one of us - young or old - who puts him- or herself at the Lord’s service can accomplish remarkable works. It’s not just for nuns and priests… It’s not just for those with a pedigree or a once-in-a-generation talent… In His saints, God shows us a panoply of ways in which people of every age, health, culture, and talent can do great things and bring others to the knowledge and love of God. From Mother Cabrini (who did much more than just encourage Charlie Greco…) to Andre Bessette (whose gentle listening healed hearts and built the largest shrine to St. Joseph in the world)... from John Henry Newman (who was one of the great thinkers in modern history) to Mother Teresa (who never had much in the way of formal education)... God isn’t limited by anything so small as our personal limitations… He’s only ever limited by our willingness to know, love and serve Him!
