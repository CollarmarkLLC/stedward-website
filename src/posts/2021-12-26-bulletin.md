## From The Font

_“Do not be afraid; for behold, I proclaim to you good news of great joy”_

It’s hard not to read these words in the voice of Linus from A Charlie Brown Christmas. Actor Christopher Shea was seven years old when he read the Gospel narrative. He died, sadly, in 2010 at the young age of 52, but his reading remains perhaps the most recognizable vocalization of the nativity story.

It’s worth remembering that most of the books of the Bible, with a handful of exceptions, began their lives as sermons. And that all the books of the Bible were read by the few and heard by the many. St. Paul in his Letter to the Romans raises exactly this point: “But how are men to call upon him in whom they have not believed? And how are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without a preacher?” (Rom 10:14)

Often, one of the best tricks that we can use to change our perspective on the Bible or on some passage is to read that passage aloud. Robin Williams in his seminal performance in Dead Poets’ Society reminds us that poetry isn’t meant to be read, it’s meant to be heard.

Christmas, after all, is consciously a reminder that our religion is sacramental. Our Faith is incarnational. That means that it’s meant to engage the whole of ourselves: body, soul, mind, and free will. When we engage with a passage of Scripture by reading it silently, we miss out on the rhythm and the poetry of the text. Even the passage I quote above has so much dynamism that is missed in a silent reading. Just take the phrase “for behold.” It has basically no meaning in a silent reading. But when read aloud, that phrase demands a long, deliberate pause in order to let the invitation for attention to settle and rouse us. It’s a profound phrase and one that calls to mind both a mystical vision and a side-show kind of huckster enthusiasm. Even the sentence itself uses the word “proclaim” which implies a public speech in which the speaker is deeply and emotionally engaged.

As we celebrate Christmas, let’s remember that we’re celebrating the bodily coming of the Lord and, in that, the fusing together for us of the spiritual with the worldly. We’re celebrating the sacramentalizing of this world.

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

**Merry Christmas!!**

I’m excited to be heading to see my parents and my sisters this Sunday. It’s been probably eight or ten years since we’ve all been together during the holidays in one place, even if it will be a brief visit. There’s a lot that can come from a sense of home that serves as potent medicine for the anxieties of the world. And that’s no small thing in the world in which we live.

Since the chaos of COVID began in March 2019 and the dramatic worsening of the situation in 2020, a shocking number of people have approached me to talk about stress and anxiety. Marriages are struggling. Individuals are showing symptoms of depression and paranoia and even some frenetic kinds of mania. And it’s not just here in Tallulah, it’s everywhere. The clinical cases of prolonged anxiety along with associated conditions are up by hundreds of percent. It’s a full-blown mental health epidemic. And to make matters worse, there’s still so much stigma around mental illness of any sort in our world and in our area in particular.

Thankfully, many of the treatments are things that we do well and that are freely available to us. We can and should spend time with others dwelling on the good rather than the bad. We can and should recount nostalgic stories and events. We can and should isolate ourselves from hysteria or paranoia - like the kind that comes from 24h news channels. We can and should pay attention to what we eat, what we watch, what we read and what we dwell on. And we can and should set aside time for prayer and for quiet mental rest.

As a pastor, my primary responsibility is spiritual. But as I harp on all the time, the incarnational nature of our Faith recognizes that the body and soul aren’t separate from the mind and the heart. And so an emotional issue is going to affect the spiritual parts of us just as much as a physical issue is going to affect the mental parts of us.

To that end, when I get back from some much needed family time, I’m going to be hosting a monthly workshop on mental and emotional health. It will be on the first Tuesday of every month in 2022. It will be in the parish hall from 6p-8p. It will be free and, while it’s certainly going to be Catholic in worldview, it’s going to be a workshop for Christians rather than a specifically Catholic religious kind of thing. I certainly want to encourage any and everyone to come and to invite friends and neighbors who may benefit from it. I’ve lined up doctors, nurses, social workers, priests, and others to give most of the talks. The first session on Jan 4 will be given by me, but most of the rest will be outside speakers.

The reality is that many of us are struggling to deal with stress and anxiety and all of us could use help - spiritually, emotionally, physically - in making good decisions and avoiding bad ones. It’s a somewhat unique moment in our lifetimes and the virtue of humility calls for us to be honest with ourselves in deciding whether we’re one of those people. I know I am.

If anyone has any questions about these workshops, please feel free to give me a call. I hope to see lots of y’all there. Until then…

**Merry Christmas & Happy New Year!**
