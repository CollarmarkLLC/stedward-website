## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

When I was in college, my parents insisted that I take a summer job. When I couldn’t find one, my dad put me to work with his construction company paving a canal in New Orleans.

During that summer, I came across one of Pope John Paul II’s books in which he tells the story of his own time working forced labor in construction under the Nazi’s. It was a profoundly moving and his insights and the way his faith gave meaning to his experience forced me to reconsider the way I thought about work in a way that has never really left me.

Now - and then - construction workers aren’t known for being polite, chaste, well-spoken or especially virtuous. That summer, I met a bunch of rough guys… But I also saw what a real work ethic does to someone. The lazy guys who showed up just to get a check and skipped out on overtime weren’t happy people. They were slaves to their situations and circumstances. Whereas, the guys who accepted where they were and worked hard and took pride in what they did were genuinely happy guys. Just like the Pope-to-be had learned in Poland, I learned in New Orleans, that Labor somehow ennobles us. Even if that labor isn’t necessarily fair or important or anything other than hard, labor has a way of ennobling us.

Later on, Pope John Paul II would write [Laborem Exercens](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_jp-ii_enc_14091981_laborem-exercens.html) - on the Dignity of Human Labor. In it, he would say that it wasn’t by accident that Adam’s purpose was to till the Earth and that it wasn’t by accident that Adam’s punishment was still to till the earth, only without the same joy or sense of purpose.

St. Benedict took that kind of thinking to heart. When he established the greatest religious order in history, he insisted on only two things: _ora_ et _labora_. Prayer and work. Every monk - according to St. Benedict - must put in six to eight hours of work, Monday through Saturday. It should be hard work - carpentry, gardening, stone-work… Only a few choir brothers were excused in order to maintain a constant vigil before the Lord in the chapel.

At Clear Creek Abbey in Eastern Oklahoma, where I make some of my retreats - the brothers fire up the organ at 4:30a to start the daily prayers. They go until 9pm with a dozen half hour breaks (praying without ceasing is serious work).

I’ve been an opponent of many of our national entitlement programs since my time as pastor of Nativity of the BVM in Campti where I saw first-hand what happens when people are told they don’t need to work. Before then, I had been a big supporter of the Great Society and the social safety nets. But it hurt my heart as a pastor to see my people sitting around all day doing nothing because if they worked or made too much money on the side, they would lose their benefits. The soul atrophies like a muscle in a cast. And people who are paid not to work… they don’t do well.

Work ennobles us. It gives us purpose. It allows us to see the fruit of our labors. It teaches us the value of delayed gratification. It teaches us patience and mental toughness. When we stop working - we get caught up in our minds. We get bored and we get addicted to entertainment and social media. In short, we begin to lose our humanity.

It’s ironic that labor day is a day of rest from our jobs. But it’s not a day of rest from our true work - the task of knowing, loving and serving God. The work of prayer never stops. The work of taking up our cross and following after Our Lord never stops.

Whatever work that we call our job or our career is not outside the reach of God. The Lord can use all things to our good and His glory and so we have to believe that our knowledge and love of God doesn’t grow only in this Church.

To the degree that we realize that God wants to use all things in life to sanctify us - to make us more like Him - to make us more fully human - we can find in our labor something truly worthy of the Lord.

Just as on religious feasts, the celebration points beyond this life to Heaven, so on this labor day, may our celebrations point beyond just the work that takes our time to the labor that ennobles us and humanizes us and that fulfills that earliest command to till the Earth and master the created world.

**A MESSAGE FROM FR ROBERT DORE,** Pastor of St. Michael, Vicksburg

Dear friends in the Lord… A few weeks ago, when I left for vacation, I was experiencing some abdominal pain. The pain continued during that time along with a loss of appetite. When I arrived back home, I made an appointment with my doctor who ordered blood work and a CT scan of my abdomen. The scan revealed that I have a mass on my liver. He ordered a liver biopsy that revealed carcinoma. The final pathological results are not available. When they become available, I will be referred to an oncologist for treatment. I have no idea what the future holds, but I know your prayers, love, and support are with me as I make this journey.
With love, prayers, and gratitude, Fr Robert
