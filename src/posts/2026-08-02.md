## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

When I was growing up, my mother used to insist that I help her cook. She baked, she stewed, and she deep-fried. In all that, I stirred, I chopped, I tasted, and I learned an awful lot about food.

To cook a brisket, you need a couple of hours. To fry a turkey, you need to start the night before. To cook some of the dishes that my mom and I made, we had to start sometimes two or three days before. Thanksgiving was a week-long affair in our kitchen. The slow work of preparation itself becomes a quiet school of patience and self-gift—the same virtues the Church asks of us when we approach the altar.

Sure, cooking is necessary to sustain life, but our Scriptures today link food and cooking and eating to something that bridges the gap between the spiritual and the physical. The prophet Isaiah cries out: “Every one who thirsts, come to the waters; and he who has no money, come, buy and eat! Come, buy wine and milk without money and without price. Why do you spend your money for that which is not bread, and your labor for that which does not satisfy? Hearken diligently to me, and eat what is good, and delight yourselves in fatness” (Is 55:1-2). God Himself sets the table and invites the hungry to feast without price.

St. Matthew shows that spiritual hunger is no less real than physical hunger. The crowds follow Jesus into the wilderness; He first teaches them, satisfying the deeper hunger of the soul, and only then multiplies the loaves and fish so that “all ate and were satisfied” (Mt 14:20). The same Lord who feeds the body also feeds the soul, and the two hungers are never finally separate.

Jesus chose to remain with us always as food. “I am the living bread which came down from heaven; if any one eats of this bread, he will live for ever; and the bread which I shall give for the life of the world is my flesh” (Jn 6:51). The Eucharist is food and more than food—the very Body, Blood, Soul, and Divinity of Christ. St. Thomas Aquinas teaches that in this sacrament “the whole Christ is contained” and that it is “the perfection of the spiritual life and the end to which all the sacraments tend” (Summa Theologiae III, q. 73, a. 3; CCC 1324). That is not something to be considered lightly.

There are two things we can take from these lessons. First, this physical world reflects and points to the spiritual world in all kinds of ways. Even something as simple as being thankful to God for a bowl of cereal in the morning can draw us closer to the Lord. St. Augustine reminds us that every created good is a trace of the Creator: “You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you” (Conf I, 1). Gratitude for daily bread becomes an act of adoration.

Second, cooking, eating, and the other works of hospitality are real Christian works. St. Benedict of Nursia insisted that “all guests who present themselves are to be welcomed as Christ, for he himself will say: I was a stranger and you welcomed me” (Rule of St. Benedict 53; cf. Mt 25:35). In the French School, St. John Eudes teaches that every act of charity done in the name of Jesus becomes a continuation of His own mission of mercy. When we set the table—for friends or family or strangers—we are doing something more than mere duty. We are doing something that is both spiritual and physical, a living icon of the banquet of the Kingdom.

It is so easy to leave the Lord here in the Church when we head home. But do not give in to that temptation. Welcome the Lord into your heart and your home. Pray before meals. Give thanks and recognize the providence of God in everything you do. St. Louis-Marie de Montfort urges us to consecrate even the ordinary moments of the day to Jesus through Mary, so that the kitchen table itself becomes an extension of the altar.

Practical step for the parish: Make a firm habit of the traditional grace before meals—“Bless us, O Lord, and these Thy gifts…”—and teach your children to pause, make the Sign of the Cross, and thank God aloud. Invite one person who might otherwise eat alone to your table this week; in that simple act you welcome Christ Himself.

Let those who are thirsty rejoice in your cooking and—at the same time—let them give thanks to God. In every shared meal, the Church’s ancient prayer is fulfilled: “You open your hand, you satisfy the desire of every living thing” (Ps 145:16).
