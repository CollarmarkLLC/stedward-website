## From The Font

_“Quick, three measures of fine flour! Knead it and make rolls… He ran to the herd, picked out a tender, choice steer, and gave it to a servant, who quickly prepared it.”_

I just can’t read passages like this without thinking about how long it took! Yeasted rolls in the ancient world would’ve taken at least three hours to knead, rise and bake. Butchering a steer and cooking steaks to a safe temperature over a wood fire would’ve taken closer to four or five hours.

Of course, the ancient world didn’t think of time the same way we do! Ancient people set aside a few days for a trip that we would drive in twenty minutes. We get upset with our waiter if we don’t have drinks and apps in ten minutes, ancient feasts lasted days with heavy drinking and simple staples like bread, nuts, olives, fruit, and pasty-textured bean dips like Hummus to fill the gaps. The meat would’ve been ready when it was ready.

Even so, the text of the first reading seems to portray the process of butchering and cooking large pieces of the steer as taking basically no time at all.

Now, I appreciate completely that this isn’t the point of this encounter between Abraham and these “angels.” But, to me, the Bible is chock full of these “ponderables.” There are so many ways in which the Lord can stimulate our minds to consider the mysteries of His creation! My undergraduate degree is in philosophy and I love history and so this question of time fascinates me. It may not be so with you, but what does fascinate you? What are those great questions that start you thinking? What little details catch you?

In teaching “Lectio Divina,” aka meditation upon the Bible, St. Ignatius of Loyola advises us to pay attention to what our minds fixate on. He tells us to slowly read through a passage of Scripture and to stop if some detail strikes us. He says that the Holy Spirit can move through that moment just as powerfully as He can move through the complexities of theology or the experiences of life.

What details in the first reading today strike you as interesting?

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

Last year, I wrote about the plenary indulgence available on August 2 for the Feast of the Portiuncula. The Portiuncula is the tiny little chapel where St. Francis of Assisi built his first little hut and began the work of “rebuilding \[the\] Church.” It’s down the hill from the little chapel of San Damiano where St. Francis had a mystical vision of Jesus who ordered the future saint to “rebuild my Church.” Francis was unsure what that meant as he strolled down the hill, he saw this little chapel that couldn’t seat more than eight people. It dated back to the 300s and the roof had collapsed. Francis took the Lord’s words literally and built himself a small hut next to the chapel and started to rebuild it brick by brick.

It was at this chapel of the “little portion” (AKA ‘porti-uncula’) that Juniper approached Francis and asked to join him in whatever it was God had called him to do. Francis agreed and what we call the Franciscans began.

For those who traveled with us to Rome in 2018, the portiuncula chapel is the one located inside the Basilica Church of the Holy Angels. That’s the location of the famous rose garden where the roses grow without thorns. It’s also the site of the gigantic nativity set which changes each year and the peculiar statue of St. Francis which has a family of doves living in it.

The indulgence is, itself, peculiar. It dates back to 1216 AD, looooong before plenary indulgences were common. St. Francis was awakened one night by the Lord and encouraged to step into his little chapel. When he did, the Lord encouraged Francis to approach the Pope about a plenary

indulgence for all who would visit the chapel and say a prayer for the Church. Pope Honorius III was hesitant, but Francis was a strange bird and everyone knew when he said “the Lord had spoken to me,” only a fool would look the other way. So Pope Honorius III established a plenary indulgence for all who would:

- Attend Mass and receive Holy Communion
- Confess their sins and be completely free from attachment to particular sin
- Pray in the chapel for the Church and specifically for the Holy Father

About a hundred years later, the lines to pass through the chapel were so crowded that some people were left without an indulgence. Later popes expanded the indulgence to be available to any who would say the requisite prayers in any Cathedral and then, later, in any Church.

An indulgence, simply put, is a remission of the punishment due to sin. Let’s say I stub my toe and a choice word passes my lips. I’m sorry about it, so I ask the Lord for forgiveness. Easy enough. BUT, all sin damages the soul. Maybe that choice word earns me an extra month or two in Purgatory. This indulgence contributes to speeding that healing and getting me out of Purgatory earlier. It doesn’t forgive my sins and if I’m not sorry for my sins, no indulgence can save me from the fires. But an Indulgence can assist me in the healing that Purgatory accomplishes and that’s why they’re a good thing to pursue.

I’m writing this year a little early so that each of us can ask any questions we have and make plans to receive the indulgence on August 2.
