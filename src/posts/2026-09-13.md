## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

Hypocrisy, when we stop and think about it in the most generic sense, is not that big of a deal. There are a ton of times where good advice that would be genuinely helpful to someone else does not apply to the one giving that advice and does not really depend upon the virtue of the one giving it.

Basically every medical appointment, every self-help program, every consultation for car repair and the like involves someone telling someone else what they ought to do without reference to the advice giver’s personal example… “You should eat less salt,” says the diabetic doctor. “You should stretch more,” says the nurse with a sore back. “You should really make a conscious effort to forgive your dad,” says the therapist with no family pictures in her office. All good advice even from whomever it comes…

But still, when religion is involved, nothing is so frustrating as hypocrisy. In the Gospel today, there is the central lesson about forgiveness, but that lesson depends upon just how annoying we find hypocrisy to be.

We could argue it’s different with religion (than it is with doctors) because we’re all held to the same standard. But that’s not true. _To whom much is given, much will be expected_. Parents of children are held to very different religious standards than a cloistered nun who has been entirely focused on prayer and holiness for the past 40 years. Their moment of eternal judgement is going to look different. And, in this life, the spiritual advice of that nun is going to be better than the advice of the busy parents. But the financial management advice of the parents will be superior to the nun’s.

Still, with religion, the underlying assumption - at least for modern people - is that all advice comes from a place of personal experience. (This is the result of the idea that Protestant Christianity is somehow “normal” or baseline Christianity.) But for all Christians, religious and spiritual truths don’t come from our personal experience, they come from Jesus… Sure, I may be able to give advice from my personal experience about how to manage human distraction in prayer, but that’s a fundamentally different kind of instruction from saying “we have to forgive even our enemies.”

And, as someone who personally struggles every day with forgiving a few specific people from my past who really tried to hurt me, I can give that advice/instruction without blushing not because I have fully and completely forgiven all MY “enemies” but because the advice is teaching that comes from Jesus, not my experience.

Hypocrisy, understood rightly, is actually a fairly specific kind of thing that Jesus uses to indict people who confuse their personal aims with the truths of the Jewish Faith. Jesus’ use of the word is almost always addressed to the professional religious people (the Scribes and Pharisees). In one place, He says “you who pack up heavy burdens for others to carry, but don’t lift a finger yourselves…” The indictment is not that they teach the truth while falling short of the glory of God themselves, it’s that they instruct or demand things which God does not.

In another place, Jesus uses the image of a splinter and a log… “Don’t try to remove the splinter from your brothers’ eye until you remove the log from your own eye.” This is the quintessential image of hypocrisy in scripture. We could modernize the idea as something like “don’t manage other peoples’ lives unless your own lives are in order!” The psychologist and controversialist Dr Jordan Peterson caused a ruckus by responding to a Gen Z question about Climate Change by saying something like: _there is absolutely a place for activism and public advocacy, but that should follow from having your own house in order. You shouldn’t be telling other people how to live their lives without knowing how to live your own well. In this way, a lot of advocacy is just avoiding dealing with your own issues. (paraphrased)_

So we end up with a nice distinction when it comes to questions of hypocrisy. On the one hand, we have our Christian obligation to share the teaching of Jesus about doctrinal and moral questions which we should do, whether or not we, ourselves, are religious or moral paragons. (Something we should be striving toward.) On the other hand, we must be cautious of advising or teaching from our own experience lest we hurt others even as we deceive ourselves.
