## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

The first existential crisis most of us face is life happens when we go to mom or dad or teacher or coach or whoever and we explain that someone didn’t treat us fairly… and we discover that the person in authority isn’t able to help. The person who lied about me or who cheated to get ahead gets away with their nefarious plan and we’re forced to realize that the world isn’t fair.

Of course, the only thing that says it should be fair is the innate part of ourselves which says the world should be as God originally planned it to be. Philosophers call that the Natural Law. Christians know that sin fundamentally changed our human nature. Sin damaged creation in a radical way. It was so destructive that the Lord God Himself promised to do away with his creation and basically to upgrade us from Eden to Heaven and from our bodies and souls as we know them to a new kind of body and soul altogether.

Today’s gospel is a bit of a gut punch to that part of ourselves that wants things to be fair and equal. To one, the master gives five talents. To another, he gives two. And to some poor sap, only one. Then, when the time comes to count out, the (pun intended) multi-talented are rewarded and the less-talented are left high and dry. Nothing about this feeds into the modern sociological drib-drab about equity, diversity, and equality of outcome. The world is unfair - by design. The Lord gives more to some than He does others and that is that.

Where Jesus adjusts the narrative is where He teaches - as He does in this gospel - that everyone has exactly what they need to live out the purpose for which they were created. Who cares if someone else’s purpose is more glorious than mine… So what if they’re the star of the show and I’m the guy who sweeps the stage… The end result of sanctity is salvation and sanctity can be achieved by all.

There’s also a powerful lesson to absorb here about the value and the benefit of UNfairness. The Lord praises poverty. The Lord favors and praises the UNinfluential and UNpowerful. St. Paul tells us that it is in weakness that we are made strong.

One of the great failures of Eve in Eden was that she trusted her gut. She was the original Disney princess who followed her heart right to her doom.

One of the most wonderful lessons of Jesus in the Gospels is that our common sense is fine for business and multiplying talents, but it’s not very good for understanding the universe and the moral law of our human nature.

In fact, having lots of talents is just fine. Having very few is fine as well. St. Ambrose, St. Augustine, Pope St. John Paul II were incredible people who were good at everything from sports to law to preaching to politics. St. Francis of Assisi was a one-trick pony - he did whatever he believed God wanted him to do in the moment. He changed the world. His religious order imploded before he died! The Franciscans today are THE example of how not to run a religious order. Our own patron King Edward the Confessor was not some brilliant polymath, he mostly did as his nobles told him to do and he took little steps when he was able. But by the Gospel standard, he put his one talent to the best use he could given his circumstances.

It’s not about how many talents, but about the willingness to use them. Look around at history. Alexander the Great was one of the most multi-talented men in world history and he didn’t do much more than war. Queen Elizabeth II of England was one of the wealthiest and most respected people to live on this planet and, beloved as she is, what has she done other than allow herself and her wealth to be reduced to little more than a mascot who reads bullet points from the prime minister and speaks in platitudes on Christmas Day before Shrek comes on…

It’s easy to misconstrue a gap in wealth or power or whatever as a function of fairness… But that understanding requires us to believe that everything in the world is without meaning and purpose. It requires us to reject that God created us and has a specific plan for each of us.

As it is, God did create us and He does imbue this world and our lives with purpose, and so we are left not to bemoan our status in wealth or health or power or whatever, but to engage as completely as possible with the Lord’s will for us.
