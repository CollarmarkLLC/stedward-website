## From The Font

_“A voice... crying out in the desert: ‘Prepare the way of the Lord, make straight his paths.’”_

One of the stranger and more spiritually dangerous ideas that has come into popular Catholicism from the charismatic protestant tradition is the idea that certain sins are demonic in nature and that we don’t have the power within ourselves to put them aside… Put another way, latent Pentecostalism has convinced some of us that we are powerless against certain kinds of sin…

And while it’s very, very true that demonic activity is real, it’s also true that we, as Christians, have the power to “Prepare the way of the Lord” and to “make straight his paths” and to “rid ourselves of EVERY burden and sin that clings to” us (Heb 12:1).

Properly understood, our sins are choices. While there are sins of the mind and sins of omission (things we should do but don’t do), all sins are choices. Bad habits and addictions are very real and very hard to overcome, but we generally call those habits “vices” rather than sins because they aren’t properly speaking free choices so much as they happen because we lack the freedom to control ourselves in their regard… That can seem like meaningless wordplay or semantics and they can keep us out of heaven just as certainly as our sins, properly speaking, can… The larger point, though, is that the Holy Spirit is ready and waiting to come to our aid if we cry out to Him.

We CAN rid ourselves of sin and vice if we really want to… NOW, the ways and means of accomplishing that freedom may be painful! I guarantee you that any one of us can lose weight or learn a new language or accomplish one of our goals if we REALLY want to! But the reason we don’t is because we don’t want to lift the weights or put aside the food or put in the practice. We excuse ourselves by talking about “willpower,” but it’s just a function of how seriously we want something.

That said, there are some great tools and techniques that are available to us to overcome our lack of want or intention and those tools may be helpful in the spiritual life as well. But none of them are more valuable than the certain truth that we CAN prepare the way and we CAN make the right choice.

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

In reading the Bible, there’s a great divide between the Old Testament and the New Testament on the topic of justice.

In the Old Testament, everyone from the Kings to the Psalmists to the Prophets to the ordinary folks want divine justice to come for them. Everybody is excited about God’s justice. In the Gospels and in the New Testament, though, everybody switches sides pretty hard and everybody is nervous about divine justice. Jesus tells us to cover our faces against the retribution of God’s justice. We are to fear justice and long, instead, for mercy. And while it’s not exactly that cut and dry… There are definitely two very different ways of thinking about divine justice.

The amazing British author CS Lewis compared the two opposing ideas to the difference between Civil and Criminal law. He says that the Jews in the Old Testament thought of God’s justice like a civil case. They were the chosen people and God had a covenant with them and so they weren’t nervous about some moral judgement at the end of days! They understood themselves to be in the legal right… When the Messiah came, He would come like a lawyer with a box of evidence and they would be vindicated against the evil Persian and Roman empires that had oppressed the Chosen People.

In the New Testament, though, CS Lewis says that Jesus teaches us to understand Judgement as a Criminal trial in which WE are the ones being judged. Judgement isn’t about being in the covenant, it’s about living up to MY PART in that covenant. And so suddenly, Judgement isn’t an easy and welcome thing, it’s a moment of existential fear.

Again, the line isn’t cut and dry and we can see some aspects of the new idea in the Old Testament and some aspects of the old idea in the New Testament, but CS Lewis’s idea is a very good analogy. It helps us to understand why Catholics don’t believe in “being saved.”

“Being Saved” in this analogy is something like a plea bargain in which we acknowledge our sin and ask forgiveness, but then we just kind of ignore everything else! If I’m saved and then I sin again, is that just not a sin? Do I not need to ask forgiveness for it? That would seem to be the Old Testament idea of judgement.

In truth, the New Testament idea of judgement is all about turning us toward God’s mercy. It’s about calling us to realize that being a disciple of Jesus isn’t about shortcuts or one-and-done decisions! That’s part of the reason prayer matters so much! Every day we’re either getting closer to Heaven or farther away! We are, indeed, adopted children of God and thus we are part of the chosen people, but the Lord’s judgement isn’t about whether we have been given a gift, it’s about what we have done with that gift! “Being saved” is a dangerous misunderstanding that John Calvin, himself, denounced near his death.

Judgement is something that we need to keep in mind if, like St. Paul prays for us “that you may be pure and blameless for the day of Christ.” It’s not about getting on the team, it’s about giving it your all in practice and on game day!
