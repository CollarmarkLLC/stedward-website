## From The Font

_“Is it lawful for a husband to divorce his wife?”_

Most modern people would answer this kind of question with references to personal “rights” and freedoms. We tend to think of the here and now almost exclusively. The idea that a moral principle would exceed and override a practical circumstance is almost unthinkable.

The Pharisees, whom Jesus is criticizing, took a similar approach. They concerned themselves entirely with the letter of the law in order to get what they wanted. Human nature hasn’t changed and so the same causes for divorce then are the causes for divorce now… A modern divorce caused by an unwise Facebook post is no different than an ancient divorce caused by a loose-tongued servant or co-worker.

As Christians, we have to look to the teachings of Jesus as truthful, helpful, and, ultimately, as freedom from our human nature’s inclinations to selfishness and sin. Modern people and ancient Pharisees both want what Eve wanted in the Garden - they want to be in charge of right and wrong… they want to be their own masters. But as Christians, we rejoice in the fact that we have but one master: Jesus! Regardless of the moral issue - divorce or honesty or overeating or pride or whatever - if I find myself trying to manipulate God or God’s law, I'm in the wrong! Let’s say that again for clarity - if I’m trying to trick God, I’m only tricking myself.

Jesus phrases the question about divorce in order to call out the Pharisees on their self-delusion. St. Paul does something similar in his letter to the Galatians where he calls them foolish for thinking that they can be saved by means of the law. The law has its value, but it can’t save. All the more, loopholes in the law are worse than useless because while they surely can’t save us, they may very well condemn us!

Every time we get one of these tough sayings of Jesus that hits some of us right between the eyes, it’s important to think first about pleasing Jesus and second about hurting or not hurting feelings! Sometimes the best thing for us is a reality check. Sometimes the best gift is tough love - from one another or from Jesus Himself. Sooner than we think, each of us is going to stand before the Lord and we’re going to want to know that we have more than the law on our side.

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

This Friday is the anniversary of the death of a wonderful and somewhat bizarre figure from our local Catholic history: Fr. François LeVozouet.

In the early 1870s, yellow fever spread across our area, killing 20-50% of the population. Shreveport was especially hard hit. In 1871, Shreveport had ~10,000 residents. By the end of 1873, it was ~5,000. As the people were dying, the priests and nuns were alongside them caring for them, anointing them, and dying with them.

The Diocese of Natchitoches had very few priests, to begin with, and as they were dying in Shreveport, the priests would write letters asking for reinforcements so that they, themselves, wouldn’t die without the Sacraments. Bishop Martín, the founding Bishop of the Diocese and a heck of a saintly bishop, did what he could. Toward the end of the epidemic (at least we know now that it was toward the end), one of the youngest priests in the diocese, Fr. LeVozouet, went to the bishop and asked to be sent to Shreveport. The Bishop made sure Fr. LeVozouet understood that he was very likely going to his death… Fr. LeVozouet assured the Bishop that he did and asked for several days to get his personal affairs in order.

When Fr. Scott Chemino told this story on the eve of the Church in Natchitoches being formally established as a Minor Basilica, he dramatically built up the narrative and explained how Fr. LeVozouet got his affairs in order, mounted his horse, and began the seventy-mile journey from Natchitoches to Shreveport. Fr. Chemino said in his own unique and inimitable voice: “Father LeVozouet set out from Natchitoches only to return two weeks later… IN A BOX!”

Indeed, Fr. LeVozouet did die in Shreveport anointing the dying and assisting with the sick even as he contracted the deadly Yellow Fever. He and four other priests of the Diocese of Natchitoches have been called the “Martyrs of Charity” ever since.

As we are in the midst of a decidedly less dangerous epidemic and as the general response of the Church has been so markedly different, history provides much wisdom. Since March 2020, I’ve thought a lot about Fr. LeVozouet and about the thousands of other priests who have run fearlessly into war zones and plague zones and ghettos and other dangerous places in an effort to save souls because they believed - as every Christian should - that we are but strangers and sojourners here in this life on Earth… This Epidemic has revealed how few of us seem to believe that this life exists only in service to the eternal life (or eternal death) which must follow it… It’s very hard to read the story of Fr. LeVozouet without comparing it to our own age and, for me at least, without comparing myself to that young priest who didn’t simply make the best of his situation but who ran knowingly and deliberately to the needs of others.

This week is an excellent time for us to ask the many, many saints who have died because they went deliberately into personal danger to pray for us that we might have their courage and faith!
