## From The Font

_“When this which is corruptible clothes itself with incorruptibility…”_

Corinth, in the time of Jesus, was akin to NYC, Paris, and Rome. It was a gigantic city in terms of population with a hugely diverse cultural mix and it was a hub for trade, wealth, education, and cutting edge science and philosophy. And so St. Paul’s letters to the Corinthians are not the folk wisdom we’re used to in the parables of Jesus… Instead, they’re sophisticated and nuanced arguments written for a relatively educated and critical audience.

Two of the worst prejudices in our modern world are against philosophy and anything old. Modern people tend to think that everything that’s not modern might as well be from the days of the cavemen. And modern people tend to think that philosophy is just long, boring books written by those old, useless people who didn’t have Facebook or Danielle Steele.

In reality, philosophy is probably the single most-important science (and it is a science) that we have as humans. We’re caught up in it every single day of our lives - even here in rural Louisiana. The study of Philosophy is like becoming psychic in that it allows us to understand the origins of the way that people think and, then, it allows us to predict behavior as well. The tyrannical actions of Trudeau in Canada were predicted by philosophers years ago because the man is a card carrying Marxist. Understanding that philosophy makes understanding his actions quite simple.

Philosophy, too, prevents us from deluding ourselves into thinking that our generation has some secret wisdom that everyone else before us failed to obtain and it makes clear that technology isn’t the paragon of wisdom or happiness that it tends to be treated as.

In Christianity, philosophy is essential. In fact, it’s so essential that priests have to get a degree in philosophy before even starting serious study of religion.

Surely, some of the books of the Bible which are more grounded in philosophy (Sirach, Wisdom, Corinthians, etc) can be much, much harder to read! But that doesn’t make them less important or less valuable - it may even make them all the the more essential.

## Thoughts from Fr. Ryan

Happy Mardi Gras!

The medieval French “Feast of Fools” and the various Western European traditions associated with the time before Lent have all blended into one to give us Carnivale - lit. “Farewell to Meat” - which culminates in the last few days before Ash Wednesday in Dimanche Gras (“Fat Sunday”), Lundi Gras (“Fat Monday”), and, of course, Mardi Gras (“Fat Tuesday”).

There are two dramatic differences in our Mardi Gras now versus years’ past. One has to do with Lent itself and the other with the advent of refrigeration.

Prior to the 1970s, Lent was hard. Catholics were obliged to abstain from meat and to eat only a single meal Monday through Saturday for all of Lent. One meal, no meat for 40 days. And that was a time when people were leaner, food variety was less, and there were no processed foods to keep you feeling full. It was real penance. Nowadays, that fasting is reduced to only two days and, sadly, very few Catholics respect Good Friday as a day of fasting. The long and short of that fasting, though, was the real need to fatten oneself up for Lent! With that kind of diet, you can easily lose 20 pounds during Lent… So Carnivale and “Fat” week was literally preparation for Lent!

The other big difference is refrigeration. Prior to the mid-1900s, food preservation was quite limited. People who lived distant from grocery stores - which was a lot of people around the world - needed to use up the preserved meat in their pantries that would likely go bad during the long meatless fast of Lent. And so the Carnivale - the farewell to meat - was a time to eat up all the delicious, high fat foods in the pantry.

There’s also a real psychological component. In days of old, people were much better equipped mentally and emotionally to dealing with fasting, penance, and to going without… Nowadays, we’re genuinely upset when our preferred brand of disposable paper product is out of stock… It’s hard for most of us to be hungry and not do something about it. Emotionally, we will do better with Lent if we indulge a bit before and then tell ourselves that my penance is not forever, it’s just for six weeks and then there’s another feast with more indulgence. It’s easier to put something off for a fixed time than it is to give it up indefinitely. And it is much easier to do that if I can tell myself that I’m refreshing the memory to store up for the lean days ahead.

Of course, Lent nowadays isn’t that hard. We have two days of fasting (Ash Wednesday and Good Friday), seven days of abstaining from meat (Ash Wednesday and each Friday of Lent), and zero Holy Days of Obligation. We’re asked to give up one food item or activity as a penance - which is not that hard. And we’re asked to be conscious of and attentive to our spiritual lives by prayer - which is something we should be doing anyway. As such, Mardi Gras doesn’t have the same value or purpose that it did in years’ past. Still, that doesn’t mean it has no value and no purpose.

Mardi Gras is a part of our shared culture and a treasured time for family and fun. I hope everyone has a good time!

## For Your Information:

WORKSHOP on DEALING WITH STRESS AND ANXIETY AS A CHRISTIAN is scheduled for this Tuesday at 6p in the Parish Hall. This month, Dr. Mimi Pham Enriquez will be speaking about the physical and psychological processes of stress and just what anxiety can do to us. Her presentation should help us to understand the real dangers of stress, especially unidentified stress, and give us some concrete ways to address it! All are welcome to attend and encouraged to invite friends who may be in need of help dealing with their own stress and anxiety.

ASH WEDNESDAY is this week. It is not a Holy Day of Obligation, but it is an excellent way to begin your Lenten season. Father Ryan will offer Mass at St. Edward at 9a as usual with the distribution of Ashes. Fr. Ryan will also distribute Ashes and offer Mass in Delhi at 6p in the evening. Remember that whether you attend Mass or not, Ash Wednesday is a day of OBLIGATORY FASTING and ABSTINENCE from meat. All Catholics over the age of 18 are obliged to eat only a single meatless meal on Ash Wednesday. If additional sustenance is needed in order to keep one’s strength or to take medicine, as many as two smaller meals may be eaten. The meals should be penitential rather than indulgent and snacking between meals is strictly forbidden.

LENT is upon us! All Catholics 18 and older are obliged to fast on Ash Wednesday (see above) and Good Friday. All Catholics 14 and older are obliged to abstain from meat on Fridays of Lent. Further, all Catholics should plan some penance (not just doing something extra) as a way of uniting ourselves with Our Lord’s death on the cross. Lent is a time for sober self-reflection, for prayer, for fasting, for works of mercy, and for redoubling our efforts to grow in the knowledge and love of God. To that end, St. Edward will offer a shared Way of the Cross on Friday evenings at 5:30p followed by Mass. On Mar 11, Mar 25 and Apr 8, immediately following the evening Mass, Fr. Ryan will host a meatless potluck (in which he will provide a hearty soup) and show a religious film. All are certainly welcome to attend.
